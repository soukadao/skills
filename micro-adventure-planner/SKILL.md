# Micro Adventure Planner

## Overview

Turn a desire for novelty into a realistic micro-adventure: small enough to do today or this week, specific enough to start, and flexible enough to preserve curiosity. Keep plans light, sensory, and constraint-aware rather than overbuilt itineraries.

## Core Principle

Micro-adventures work best when they change one or two variables in ordinary life:

- place: a new street, station, park, store, library, shrine, cafe, view, route, or neighborhood
- sense: taste, sound, smell, texture, light, weather, movement, or color
- role: photographer, collector, interviewer, sketcher, food explorer, local tourist, quiet observer
- social pattern: solo, one friend, casual group, call-walk, or shared challenge
- time shape: sunrise, lunch break, golden hour, night walk, rainy-day errand, 30-minute detour

For ENFP-style planning, protect the spark while adding a small amount of structure: offer choices, keep the first step easy, and include a simple ending ritual so the adventure feels complete.

## Workflow

1. Capture the user's current state.
   - Note mood, energy, available time, budget, weather, social preference, mobility, distance tolerance, and any hard commitments.
   - If location-specific suggestions are needed and the location is missing, ask for a city or neighborhood. If not needed, give location-agnostic patterns.

2. Choose an adventure mode.
   - `solo reset`: quiet novelty, recovery, wandering, sensory grounding.
   - `social spark`: low-pressure connection, shared discovery, easy conversation.
   - `creative hunt`: photos, sketches, sounds, textures, signs, colors, stories.
   - `body shift`: walking, cycling, gentle movement, stairs, waterfront, parks.
   - `taste detour`: one food, drink, bakery, market, convenience-store comparison, picnic.
   - `local tourist`: familiar area with an unfamiliar rule or route.

3. Generate 3-5 options.
   - Vary effort level and social intensity.
   - Keep each option concrete: where to go, what to do, what to notice, and how long it takes.
   - Include a fallback that works at home or within a short walk.

4. Pick one recommended plan.
   - Choose the option that best matches the user's current energy and constraints.
   - Keep the plan short: start trigger, route/action, tiny mission, optional extension, and stop point.

5. Add friction guards.
   - If the user tends to over-plan, limit the plan to one destination and one mission.
   - If the user tends to cancel when energy drops, provide a 10-minute version.
   - If the user tends to keep wandering too long, add a return cue or closing time.
   - If spending is a concern, give a no-buy or low-buy variant.

## Output Shape

Use concise Japanese when the user writes Japanese. Prefer this format:

## Idea Bank

- Walk a route chosen by one constraint: follow shade, water, red signs, quiet streets, stairs, or old buildings.
- Buy or make one small drink and consume it somewhere unusual but comfortable.
- Visit a familiar place with a role: photographer, notebook keeper, color hunter, sound collector, or local guide.
- Take a one-station detour and find one detail worth telling someone about.
- Create a tiny field note: three photos, three words, one sentence, or one sketch.
- Pair a necessary errand with one novelty rule, such as a new route or one unfamiliar shop.
- Turn a friend meetup into a small mission: compare two snacks, find the best bench, or choose a random street.

## Safety And Fit

- Do not recommend unsafe areas, trespassing, risky weather exposure, illegal activity, or activities that require hidden costs.
- When the plan involves physical effort, scale it to the user's stated health, mobility, and energy.
- When local conditions matter and are unknown, suggest checking weather, transit, opening hours, and daylight before leaving.
- Avoid turning the adventure into a productivity challenge. The goal is renewal, curiosity, and a small memorable moment.
